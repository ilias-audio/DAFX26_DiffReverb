Ilias Ibnyahya and Joshua D. Reiss, Centre for Digital Music, Queen Mary University of London

Summary

There are two common ways to add reverb. Convolving with a measured room impulse response (RIR) sounds exact, but it is heavy and fixed. A feedback delay network (FDN) is light and adjustable, but hard to match to a real room. We get both: we fit a fully differentiable FDN to a measured RIR by gradient descent.

The FDN has 16 delay lines at 48 kHz. Everything is trained at once: the delay lengths, the feedback matrix, the early-reflection taps, and the filters that shape how each frequency decays.

One set of parametric-EQ (PEQ) bands is shared by all delay lines, and each line's gain follows from Jot's proportionality rule. For the same number of parameters this beats graphic-EQ filters on reverberation-time (T₃₀) error, while using 2.8× fewer multiplies per sample and training several times faster.

The loss is built around an Energy Decay Relief (EDR) term, with extra terms for T₃₀, direct-to-reverberant ratio, early energy, and echo density. The result runs at about the per-sample cost of partitioned convolution while using roughly 11× less memory.

Built for gradient-based training. The whole model is differentiable, so it drops into any PyTorch / autograd pipeline as a trainable layer. Each optimization step is a single forward-and-backward pass over the (impulse, target) pair at 48 kHz: about 0.2 s for a typical room and 0.3 s averaged over the nine rooms on one consumer GPU (RTX 4070 Super). A full fit is 300 steps in under two minutes. With only 494 trainable parameters and about 1,222 multiplies per output sample, both the forward and backward passes stay cheap, which makes it practical to fit at scale or embed inside a larger learned audio system.

A full-size, fully differentiable FDN: every parameter trainable, fit to a measured room in ~300 gradient steps (under two minutes) on one GPU — a drop-in layer for audio-ML pipelines.

The noise-shaped baseline wins on broadband EDR because it fills the tail with dense noise, but it has no real early reflections or direct sound, which you can hear in DRR and in the examples below. On everything else the proposed FDN is the strongest of the four, and it stays fully parametric.

Audio examples

Pick a room and compare the columns. The reference is the measured RIR, and every other column is a fitted model. Rows are the raw impulse response and six dry sounds convolved with it. One clip plays at a time, and switching method within a row keeps the playback position, so you hear the same moment through each reverberator.

The highlighted column is the proposed FDN: every parameter trainable, about two minutes per room. The column tagged recommended keeps the delays fixed and trains the rest, which is the most reliable setting in practice.

Playback here is 320 kbps MP3. Lossless 48 kHz WAV impulse responses — the measured targets and the kept models — are in docs/ir_wav/ in the repository.

Spectrum of the fit —

Magnitude spectra (1/6-octave smoothed) of the measured target and the proposed all-diff FDN, with their difference on the right. The difference shows exactly where the model is still too loud or too quiet: the mid band matches within a couple of dB, and the residual concentrates at the spectral edges. These update with the room selected above.

Full time–frequency–energy spectrograms of the same target and fit. Time runs left to right, frequency (log) bottom to top, and colour is energy in dB. The difference map on the right shows where in time and frequency the model has too much or too little energy: red is too loud, blue too quiet.

Early-time structure, zoomed to the first ~150 ms with t = 0 at the direct sound. You can see the first impulse, the individual early reflections (orange — the model's ER branch) and the late reverberation (green — the recursive FDN branch) building up underneath, over the measured target (grey). This is where the direct sound, early reflections, and diffuse late field hand off to one another.
